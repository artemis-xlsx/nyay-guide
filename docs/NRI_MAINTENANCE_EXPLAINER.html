What Is Nyay Guide & How Should Practitioners Use It? Nyay Guide is an open **scenario-planning and legal research framework**, not an automated predictive engine that forecasts what an individual Family Court judge will decide. In Indian family jurisprudence, the award of interim or permanent maintenance under **Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS**, **Section 24 & 25 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955**, or **Section 20 of the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005** is an exercise of judicial discretion grounded in equity and the specific facts of each case. Nyay Guide assists advocates and litigants by: 1. **Structuring complex cross-border facts**: Capturing transnational matrimonial abandonment, foreign jurisdiction, currency conversion, and undisclosed overseas salaries. 2. **Modeling financial scenarios**: Allowing advocates to test alternative host-country living deductions (30%, 45%, or proven actuals) and spousal maintenance proportions (20%, 25%, 33%). 3. **Evaluating private international law defenses**: Automatically auditing foreign divorce decrees against the statutory criteria of **Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC)**. 4. **Mapping realistic cross-border enforcement remedies**: Distinguishing between reciprocating territories (UK, UAE, Singapore) where **Section 44A CPC** direct execution lies, and non-reciprocating jurisdictions (USA, Canada, Australia) where fresh civil suits or coercive domestic remedies are mandatory. --- ## 2. The Core Legal Formulas & Their Critical Judicial Limitations ### 1. Overseas Living-Cost & Tax Deductions: A Configurable Scenario, Not a Statutory Mandate - **Judicial Context**: In *Devika Jain v. Rajesh Jain* (2015 SCC OnLine Del 13245) and *Shikha v. Avaneesh Mahodaya* (2024 SCC OnLine MP 5791), High Courts observed that when an Indian spouse earns in foreign currency (e.g. US Dollars or British Pounds), Indian trial courts cannot mechanically convert the gross foreign earnings at spot exchange rates into Indian Rupees without accounting for local taxes and high living expenditures in the host country. - **The Misconception**: Some commentators assume Indian law entitles every NRI to an automatic "flat 50% deduction" across the board. - **The Correct Legal Position**: - There is **no statutory 50% deduction**. - The permissible deductions must be established by evidence of actual host-country taxes (e.g. US Federal/State income tax, UK PAYE/NIC), mandatory social security contributions, housing rent, and healthcare costs. - In Nyay Guide, the living cost deduction is **fully configurable by the user or advocate** (e.g. 0% for domestic earnings, 30% for low-tax Gulf jurisdictions, 40–45% for high-tax Western nations, or an exact rupee amount based on filed tax returns). ### 2. The 25% Net Salary Benchmark: An Indicative Guideline, Not a Universal Cap - **Judicial Context**: In *Kalyan Dey Chowdhury v. Rita Dey Chowdhury Nee Nandy* ((2017) 14 SCC 200), the Supreme Court referenced its earlier 3-judge bench ruling in *Dr. Kulbhushan Kumar v. Raj Kumari* ((1970) 3 SCC 129) and observed that an award of **25% of the husband's net salary** was just and proper maintenance for the wife in the facts of that case. - **The Misconception**: Treating 25% as a binding nationwide ceiling or an automatic universal entitlement. - **The Correct Legal Position**: - The Supreme Court's 25% benchmark is an **indicative guideline**, not a rigid statutory entitlement or cap. - Trial judges retain plenary discretion under the nationwide guidelines in *Rajnesh v. Neha* ((2021) 2 SCC 324) to calibrate maintenance higher or lower based on the parties' standard of living during cohabitation, the claimant's medical needs, dependent children's schooling, and independent income. - In Nyay Guide, the spousal percentage can be modeled at 20%, 25%, 33%, or custom proportions. ### 3. Adverse Inference on Non-Disclosure: Realistic Imputation vs. Arbitrary Guesswork - **Judicial Context**: When an NRI spouse resides abroad and conceals their income, tax returns, and passport details, the Kerala High Court in *Baiju v. Hema* (2020 SCC OnLine Ker 12784) ruled that trial courts must invoke **Section 114(g) Evidence Act (now Section 119(g) BSA)** and draw an adverse inference. Because foreign salary records are within the husband's exclusive knowledge (**Section 106 Evidence Act / Section 109 BSA**), non-production justifies the court in assessing income based on qualifications and lifestyle. - **The Misconception**: Assuming the court will automatically accept any astronomical salary claimed by the petitioner without evidence. - **The Correct Legal Position**: - As held in *Baiju v. Hema*, courts cannot pluck arbitrary or fanciful figures out of thin air. - The adverse inference must be anchored in objective circumstantial indicators: educational qualifications (e.g. IIT/IIM/MS degrees), visa category (e.g. H-1B, Tier 1, Golden Visa), standard of living during marriage, foreign country of residence, and credible industry benchmarks. - Nyay Guide distinguishes between: 1. *Disclosed & Verified Income* (backed by Form 1040, W-2, P60, or bank records). 2. *Conservative Market Benchmarks* (dated salary medians for specific professions). 3. *Concealed / Unknown Income* subject to judicial adverse inference. ### 4. Interim Sustenance Floor & The Able-Bodied Husband Doctrine - **Judicial Context**: In *Kulwinder Kumar v. Seema* (2017 SCC OnLine P&H 5218), the Punjab & Haryana High Court affirmed that an able-bodied husband working in Europe cannot plead irregular wages or foreign financial hardship to deny interim sustenance to his abandoned wife in India. - **The Misconception**: Believing there is a binding nationwide statutory minimum floor of ₹20,000–₹35,000 for all NRIs. - **The Correct Legal Position**: - Indian law contains no statutory dollar or rupee floor for NRI maintenance. - The "able-bodied husband doctrine" ensures that an able-bodied spouse capable of earning cannot leave the other spouse destitute. The interim maintenance amount is calibrated to ensure basic dignity and survival during trial. - In Nyay Guide, any interim sustenance floor is treated as an **optional illustrative scenario**, not a guaranteed statutory entitlement. --- ## 4. Private International Law: Foreign Divorce Recognition Under Section 13 CPC One of the most frequent tactics deployed by defaulting NRI spouses is to secure an ex-parte foreign divorce decree abroad and plead that the marriage is dissolved, arguing that the Indian court has lost jurisdiction. Under Indian private international law, the conclusiveness of foreign judgments is governed strictly by **Section 13 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC)**. A foreign divorce decree is **not conclusive in India** if it falls under any of the following six exceptions: ``` ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ Section 13 CPC: Conclusiveness of Foreign Decrees │ └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ │ ┌───────────────────┬───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┬───────────────────┐ ▼ ▼ ▼ ▼ ▼ Section 13(a) Section 13(b) Section 13(c) Section 13(d) Section 13(e) Court Lacked True Decree Not on Merits Refusal to Apply Proper Opposed to Natural Obtained by Fraud Jurisdiction (Ex-Parte Default) Indian Personal Law Justice on Jurisdiction (Fleeting Stay) (e.g. Irretrievable (Wife Unable to (Satya v. Teja) Breakdown) Contest) ``` ### The Section 13 CPC Checklist: 1. **Section 13(a) — Incompetent Jurisdiction**: The foreign court must possess competent jurisdiction under Indian private international law rules. Transient or temporary residence (e.g. a 6-week stay in Nevada as in *Satya v. Teja Singh*) does not confer competent jurisdiction. 2. **Section 13(b) — Decision Not on the Merits**: Where a foreign divorce is granted summarily or ex-parte due to non-appearance or procedural default without taking evidence on the statutory grounds of divorce, it is unenforceable in India. 3. **Section 13(c) — Refusal to Recognize Indian Personal Law**: As definitively established by the 3-judge bench in *Y. Narasimha Rao v. Y. Venkata Lakshmi* ((1991) 3 SCC 451), a Hindu marriage solemnized in India can only be dissolved on grounds recognized under the **Hindu Marriage Act, 1955**. If a foreign court dissolves the marriage on a foreign ground not recognized by Indian law (such as "irretrievable breakdown of marriage" under Missouri or UK law) without voluntary submission by the wife, the decree is a nullity in India. 4. **Section 13(d) — Proceedings Opposed to Natural Justice**: Where the wife in India was served with process under circumstances where she had neither the financial resources, visa clearance, nor opportunity to contest the overseas action. 5. **Section 13(e) — Judgment Obtained by Fraud**: In *Satya v. Teja Singh* ((1975) 1 SCC 120; Criminal Appeal No. 187 of 1970, decided 1 October 1974), the Supreme Court struck down a Nevada divorce decree because the husband practiced fraud upon the foreign court by masquerading transient residence as permanent domicile. 6. **Section 13(f) — Claim Founded on Breach of Indian Law**: Foreign decree cannot sustain claims violating Indian public policy. > **Legal Consequence**: If the foreign decree fails under any single ground of Section 13 CPC, the marital tie legally subsists in India. The husband remains bound by statutory maintenance obligations under Section 125 CrPC / Section 144 BNSS, and any subsequent remarriage by the husband constitutes bigamy under Section 494 IPC. --- ## 5. Cross-Border Enforcement: Realities, Bottlenecks & Execution Routes Winning an Indian maintenance order on paper is only half the battle. Enforcing it across international borders requires navigating complex statutory boundaries: ### 1. Section 44A CPC: Reciprocating vs. Non-Reciprocating Nations Section 44A of the CPC provides a special mechanism allowing certified decrees of superior courts of "reciprocating territories" to be executed directly in an Indian District Court as if passed by an Indian court: - **Reciprocating Territories (Direct Execution Permitted)**: - **United Kingdom** - **United Arab Emirates (UAE)** — *Formally gazetted via Notification S.O. 261(E) dated 17.01.2020* - **Singapore** - **New Zealand, Fiji, Hong Kong, Malaysia** - *Mechanism*: A certified copy of the decree can be transmitted through official diplomatic channels for direct execution against assets in the foreign reciprocating country. - **Non-Reciprocating Territories (USA, Canada, Australia) — DIRECT EXECUTION BARRED**: - The United States, Canada, and Australia are **NOT** notified reciprocating territories under Section 44A CPC. - **The Legal Reality**: An Indian maintenance order or decree **cannot be directly executed** in the USA, Canada, or Australia under Section 44A. - **Remedy**: The decree-holder must file a **fresh civil suit on the foreign judgment** in the appropriate foreign court within the foreign statute of limitations (or in India under Article 101 of the Limitation Act, 1963 within 3 years). Because of the prohibitive cost of foreign litigation (\$300–\$600/hour for US/Canadian attorneys), most litigants rely on **domestic coercive remedies** against the NRI's person and Indian assets. ### 2. Domestic Coercive Remedies in India When foreign enforcement is procedurally blocked or financially unfeasible, Indian courts utilize powerful domestic remedies: 1. **Passport Impoundment (*Passports Act Section 10(3)(h)*)**: - In *Suresh Nanda v. CBI* ((2008) 3 SCC 674), the Supreme Court clarified that criminal courts and police do **not** possess the statutory power to permanently impound passports under Section 104 CrPC. - However, the Family Court or Magistrate can issue a judicial requisition directing the **Regional Passport Officer (RPO)** or Ministry of External Affairs to initiate proceedings under **Section 10(3)(h) of the Passports Act, 1967** to impound or revoke the passport of an absconding NRI spouse against whom summons or warrants are pending. 2. **Look Out Circulars (LOC)**: - Issued under the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA) Consolidated Guidelines OM dated 22.02.2021. - The court or police can request the Bureau of Immigration (BOI) to open an LOC. When the NRI spouse lands at an Indian international airport for vacation or family visits, immigration authorities detain them or prevent their departure until maintenance arrears are secured. 3. **Attachment of Indian & Ancestral Properties (*Section 147 BNSS / Section 128 CrPC*)**: - The court can attach the defaulting spouse's bank accounts, residential properties, and undivided coparcenary share in ancestral Hindu Undivided Family (HUF) properties located in India. 4. **Anti-Suit Injunctions**: - As affirmed in *Jasmeet Kaur v. Navtej Singh* ((2018) 4 SCC 295) and *Harmeeta Singh v. Rajat Taneja* (2003 SCC OnLine Del 60), Indian High Courts possess equitable jurisdiction to issue anti-suit injunctions restraining an NRI husband from pursuing oppressive and vexatious foreign divorce proceedings abroad. --- ## 6. Frequently Asked Questions (Litigant Q&A) ### Q1: My husband is in Dubai / USA and refuses to give his pay stubs. How will the court calculate maintenance? Under *Rajnesh v. Neha* ((2021) 2 SCC 324) and *Aditi v. Jitesh Sharma* (2023 SCC OnLine SC 1451), filing a sworn **Affidavit of Assets and Liabilities** is legally mandatory. If the husband refuses to disclose his foreign income, the court will invoke **Section 119(g) BSA / Section 114(g) Evidence Act** (*Baiju v. Hema*) to draw an **adverse inference**. The judge will estimate his income based on his educational degrees, LinkedIn profile, visa type, lifestyle photos, and market benchmarks for tech/medical/managerial roles in his city of residence. ### Q2: He obtained an ex-parte divorce decree in a US or UK court. Is our marriage over? In almost all cases where the wife remained in India and did not voluntarily appear and contest on the merits, the foreign decree is **void in India** under **Section 13 CPC** (*Satya v. Teja Singh*, *Y. Narasimha Rao*). The foreign court had no jurisdiction to dissolve a marriage solemnized under Hindu law on grounds like "irretrievable breakdown." Your marriage remains valid under Indian law, and your maintenance rights are fully preserved. ### Q3: I work in India and earn a modest salary (e.g. ₹30,000/month). Am I disqualified from claiming maintenance? **No.** As settled by the Supreme Court in *Manish Jain v. Akanksha Jain* ((2017) 15 SCC 801) and *Sunita Kachwaha v. Anil Kachwaha* ((2014) 16 SCC 715), a wife's independent income or educational qualifications do not disentitle her from maintenance. Maintenance is designed to ensure the claimant enjoys a standard of living reasonably commensurate with the status and wealth of the overseas spouse. Her earnings are simply factored into the final quantum assessment. ### Q4: Can a divorced wife claim maintenance if she has not remarried? **Yes.** Under Explanation (b) to **Section 125(1) CrPC (now Section 144(1) BNSS)** and *Moni v. Sujatha* (1986 KLT 257), the definition of "wife" explicitly includes a woman who has been divorced by, or has obtained a divorce from, her husband and has not remarried. Past marital allegations cannot be used to defeat post-divorce maintenance rights. --- ## 7.
